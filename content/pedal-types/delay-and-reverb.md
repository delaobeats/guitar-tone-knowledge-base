# Delay and Reverb

Delay and reverb both add a sense of space, yet they create that space in different ways. **Delay** repeats a note or phrase at a chosen interval, like an echo. **Reverb** produces a collection of reflections that suggest a room, hall, or imagined environment. Both effects are important for atmospheric guitar sounds, but too much of either can cover up the original performance.

## Understanding Delay Controls

- **Time:** The gap between the original note and its repeat.
- **Feedback:** How many repeats continue after the note.
- **Mix:** How loud the affected signal is compared with the dry guitar.

Tempo matters when repeating rhythmic patterns. At 120 BPM, one quarter note lasts 500 milliseconds. Setting a delay around that value creates a repeat that lands on each beat. Other subdivisions can create faster movement without changing how quickly you pick.

### Starting With a Clean Phrase

Play four slow notes and set the mix fairly low. Increase the feedback until the repeats are easy to notice, then reduce it until they support the phrase instead of fighting it. Repeat the test with the [[pedal-types/overdrive-and-distortion|drive tone]] you would use in a song. High-gain sounds can make repeated notes blend together more quickly.

## Reverb: Choosing a Space

A short room reverb can make a dry guitar feel more natural. A long hall or ambient reverb can stretch chords into a background texture. Decay controls how long the reverb continues, while mix controls how prominently the effect appears. For a layered arrangement, a little reverb on rhythm guitars and more on a separate ambient part can keep the mix clearer.

> Space is most effective when the listener can still hear the note that created it.
